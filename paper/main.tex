\begin{abstract}
% SEP 18 VERSION (no results yet). Replace with the full version from the plan once numbers exist.
Gated linear units (GLUs) are the standard feed-forward block of modern Transformer language models and
hold most of each block's parameters in three equally sized projections: a gate, an up-projection, and a
down-projection. These are commonly described as doing different jobs: the gate selects which hidden
units are active, while the up- and down-projections carry the content written back to the residual
stream. Standard architectures nevertheless give all three the same capacity. We ask whether selection
is cheaper than content. Because the three matrices share a shape, the question admits a controlled
test: restrict one projection at a time to the same rank, so that parameter savings are identical, and
measure which restriction the model tolerates best. First, in open pretrained language models and
without any training, we truncate each projection with plain and activation-aware singular value
decompositions and compare perplexity. Second, in small-scale pretraining from scratch with multiple
seeds, we compare the thin-gate GLU, which factorizes only the gate and keeps the content path dense,
against dense, width-reduced, and uniformly low-rank baselines at matched parameter counts, and against
the symmetric controls that factorize only the up- or only the down-projection. Third, we test whether
pretrained models can be converted by truncating one projection and training only the new factors. We
report measured throughput and memory alongside parameter and FLOP counts, and state the scale limits of
our experiments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{GLU feed-forward block.} For an input $\vx \in \R^{d}$, a SwiGLU block with hidden width
$d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ computes
\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{FFN}(\vx) = \mW_{\mathrm{down}} \big( \sigma(\mW_{\mathrm{gate}} \vx) \odot (\mW_{\mathrm{up}} \vx) \big),
\end{equation}
with $\mW_{\mathrm{gate}}, \mW_{\mathrm{up}} \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{ff}} \times d}$,
$\mW_{\mathrm{down}} \in \R^{d \times d_{\mathrm{ff}}}$, and $\sigma$ the SiLU function. All three matrices
have $d \, d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ parameters.

\paragraph{Thin-gate GLU.} We replace only the gate by a rank-$r$ factorization,
$\mW_{\mathrm{gate}} = \mB \mA$ with $\mA \in \R^{r \times d}$ and $\mB \in \R^{d_{\mathrm{ff}} \times r}$,
and keep $\mW_{\mathrm{up}}$ and $\mW_{\mathrm{down}}$ dense. The gate then costs $r(d + d_{\mathrm{ff}})$
parameters (and multiply-accumulates per token) instead of $d\,d_{\mathrm{ff}}$, so the block shrinks from
$3 d\, d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ to $2 d\, d_{\mathrm{ff}} + r (d + d_{\mathrm{ff}})$. No nonlinearity is inserted between
$\mA$ and $\mB$. Both factors are initialised from a zero-mean normal distribution with standard deviation
$(s^2 / r)^{1/4}$, so that the entries of $\mB\mA$ have the same variance $s^2$ as the dense initialisation.

\paragraph{Controls.} \emph{Thin-up} and \emph{thin-down} apply the same rank-$r$ factorization to
$\mW_{\mathrm{up}}$ or $\mW_{\mathrm{down}}$ instead; because the three matrices have the same shape these
controls have exactly the same parameter count as thin-gate. \emph{Shrunk} is a dense GLU with reduced
$d_{\mathrm{ff}}$; \emph{all-low-rank} factorizes all three matrices at one rank; both are matched to the
thin-gate parameter count (within \matchGapPct\%). \emph{Reinvest} is a thin-gate GLU whose $d_{\mathrm{ff}}$ is
widened until it matches the dense parameter count.

\paragraph{Training-free truncation.} For a pretrained model we replace one projection type in every layer by
its best rank-$r$ approximation and measure perplexity. \emph{Plain} truncation uses the SVD of $\mW$.
\emph{Activation-aware} truncation minimises $\|(\mW - \mW')\mX\|_F$ over calibration inputs $\mX$: with the
Cholesky factor $\mS\mS^\top = \mX\mX^\top$ we truncate the SVD of $\mW\mS$ and multiply by $\mS^{-1}$.

\section{Experiments}
\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Training-free truncation (Exp.~A).} We use seven open pretrained GLU language models spanning
135M to 1.7B parameters: SmolLM2-135M, -360M and -1.7B, Qwen2.5-0.5B and -1.5B, TinyLlama v1.1 (1.1B) and
OLMo-2-1B. % CITE each model's release paper or model card after reading it.
For each model and each projection type (gate, up, down) we replace that projection in every layer by its
rank-$r$ approximation, for $r/d \in \{3/4, 1/2, 3/8, 1/4, 1/8, 1/16\}$ where $d$ is the model width, and
measure perplexity on the full WikiText-2 test split in non-overlapping windows of 1024 tokens.
Activation-aware truncation is calibrated on 32 windows of the WikiText-2 training split. All Exp.~A runs
are in float32 on NVIDIA T4 GPUs. Because the three projections of a block have the same shape, a given
rank $r$ removes the same number of parameters whichever projection it is applied to; the parameter count
of the factorized projection relative to the dense one is $r(d + d_{\mathrm{ff}}) / (d\, d_{\mathrm{ff}})$
and is reported alongside $r/d$ (Figure~\ref{fig:posthoc_params}).

\paragraph{Pretraining from scratch (Exp.~B).} We train decoder-only Transformers (pre-norm with RMSNorm,
rotary positions, SwiGLU blocks, input and output embeddings tied, the GPT-2 vocabulary) on
GPT-2-tokenized FineWeb. % CITE FineWeb and the tokenized release after reading them.
Size S has \nLayerS{} layers, \nHeadS{} heads, width $d = \dModelS$ and dense
$d_{\mathrm{ff}} = \dFFS$ (\denseTotalM{}M parameters, \denseNonEmbedM{}M outside the embedding). Every arm
sees the same \trainTokensM{}M tokens in the same order: \trainSteps{} steps of \batchTokens{} tokens in
sequences of \seqLen{}, AdamW with $(\beta_1, \beta_2) = (0.9, 0.95)$, peak learning rate \lrPeak{} after
\warmupSteps{} warm-up steps with cosine decay to \lrFloor{}, weight decay \weightDecay{} on matrices only,
gradient clipping at \gradClip{}. The seed fixes both the initialisation and the data order, so arms that share
a seed see identical batches and every comparison below is paired by seed; we use \nSeeds{} seeds per arm.
We report the cross-entropy on a fixed held-out set of \evalTokensK{}K FineWeb tokens after the last step.
All hyperparameters were fixed before the first arm ran and are identical across arms; the method received
no tuning the baseline did not. Runs use \ampDtype{} autocast with loss scaling on \gpuName{} GPUs (no

Three regularities hold across the seven models. First, the up-projection is the most fragile of the three:
in all but one model-rank setting, truncating the up-projection costs more perplexity than truncating the
gate to the same rank, usually far more, and in the one exception the two are within a fraction of a
percent of each other. \todo{verify the exception against the final table: SmolLM2-360M at $r/d=1/4$.}
Second, at $r/d = 3/4$ the gate is the projection every model tolerates best, and at $r/d = 1/2$ this holds
for all but one model, where gate and down-projection are within a few percent. \todo{verify: Qwen2.5-0.5B.}
Third, the ranking of gate and down-projection depends on the rank: at aggressive ranks ($r/d \le 1/4$)
the down-projection overtakes the gate in most models. The hypothesis that selection is cheaper than
content therefore holds only in part: the gate is cheaper than the up-projection throughout, and cheaper
than the down-projection at moderate rank.

\subsection{Pretraining from scratch}
\label{sec:expb}

Figure~\ref{fig:training} and Table~\ref{tab:training} give the final validation loss of every arm at size S,
and Table~\ref{tab:paired} the seed-paired differences on which the conclusions rest.

\paragraph{Noise floor.} The three dense seeds span \denseRange{} in final loss (mean \denseMean), set by
one seed that trails the other two from early in training on. The low-rank arms are far more consistent
across seeds (Table~\ref{tab:training}). We therefore state every comparison as a seed-paired difference and
call a gap an effect only if it has the same sign in all three pairs.

\paragraph{The thin gate never beats the narrower dense block.} At every rank tried ($r/d = 1/2, 1/4,
1/8$) and at every seed, the thin-gate model ends with a higher loss than the dense block of the same
parameter count (\emph{shrunk}); all nine paired differences in Table~\ref{tab:paired} are positive. The
gap is about the same at $r/d = 1/2$ and $1/4$ and roughly doubles at $1/8$: the thinner the gate, the
further it falls behind. At $r/d = 1/4$ the mean losses are \meanThinGateRFour{} (thin gate) against
\meanShrunkRFour{} (shrunk).

\paragraph{Gate and up-projection are interchangeable; the down-projection is not.} Thin-gate and thin-up
end within noise of each other: the paired difference changes sign across seeds and its mean is smaller
than every individual dense-seed gap (Table~\ref{tab:paired}). Thin-down is clearly the worst of the three
single-projection arms, behind shrunk in every pair by three to four times the thin-gate gap, and
factorizing all three projections at once is worse still (\meanAllLowrankRFour).

\paragraph{The dense block is wider than it needs to be at this scale.} Shrunk at $r/d = 1/2$, with about
ten percent fewer MLP parameters than dense, ends at \meanShrunkRTwo{} against \denseMean{} for dense: the
paired differences straddle zero. \emph{Reinvest}, a thin gate widened back to the dense parameter count,
ends at \meanReinvestRFour{}, within the dense seed range. Spending the gate's savings on width neither beats
nor clearly loses to dense.

\paragraph{Alternatives to a low-rank gate.} Because a rank-$r$ gate confines every unit's key to one
$r$-dimensional subspace, we also screened, at the same parameter count, gates that are cheap in other
ways. A first group (seed 0 only) changed the low-rank gate's initialisation or weight decay, made it
block-structured (Monarch), let one gate value serve four hidden units, inserted a nonlinearity between the
factors, or truncated a dense gate part-way through training; none ends below shrunk (Appendix,
Table~\ref{tab:screen}). A second group removed the gate's parameters instead of reducing their rank:
\emph{tied} gates reuse the up-projection, $\mathrm{FFN}(\vx) = \mW_{\mathrm{down}}(\phi(\vz) \odot \vz)$
with $\vz = \mW_{\mathrm{up}}\vx$, so every unit gates itself and the saved parameters widen the block to
$d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 1200$; \emph{thin+tied} adds a per-unit multiple of $\vz$ to a rank-$r$ gate at the
original width; a \emph{shared} gate uses one gate matrix for all layers.

\paragraph{A gate without parameters beats the narrower dense block.} With $\phi = \mathrm{relu}$, so that
each hidden unit computes $\mathrm{relu}(z)^2$, the tied block ends at \meanTiedGateRelu{} against
\meanShrunkRFour{} for shrunk, ahead at every seed by more than twice the largest thin-gate deficit
(Table~\ref{tab:paired}), and it is also ahead of the dense block at every seed with 78\% of its MLP
parameters. Thin+tied with $\phi = \mathrm{silu}$ ends at \meanThinTiedGateRFour{}, ahead of shrunk at every
seed and within noise of dense; it beats the plain thin gate of the same rank by more than the dense seed
range, so the per-unit self term is what the low-rank gate was missing. The silu-tied block and the shared
gate were behind shrunk at 44\% of training and were stopped by a pre-registered rule (appendix). At a
starved budget ($d_{\mathrm{ff}} = 400$ for shrunk, 600 for the silu-tied block) the two tie
(Table~\ref{tab:starved}). \todo{The relu-tied block is exactly Primer's squared ReLU (So et al.\ 2021 state the equivalence with a
weight-tied ReGLU) and its advantage over SwiGLU at equal parameters is reported there at 110M and in the
xIELU paper at 1.1B; this paragraph must say so once both are read. What is ours: the controlled grid around
it, the reason the low-rank gate fails, and the context-thresholded block (session 7: rank sweep of the
context term at matched parameters, plus the relu-gate control).}

\paragraph{Reading Exp.~A and Exp.~B together.} The projection that tolerates truncation best after
training (the gate, Section~\ref{sec:expa}) is not the one that trains best at low rank (gate and
up-projection tie, both behind a narrower dense block), and the projection most fragile after training (up)
is not the one that hurts most when trained thin (down). Post-hoc rank tolerance does not predict
from-scratch trainability, in either direction. \todo{Tokens/s and memory from bench.py, once run; the
throughput logged during training is not a speed measurement because two runs shared each GPU.}
